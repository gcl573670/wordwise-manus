# Their vs There vs They’re: How to Choose the Right Word

*Their*, *there*, and *they’re* sound alike in most varieties of English, but they do different jobs in a sentence. The quick distinction is: **their** shows belonging or association, **there** points to a place or helps introduce what exists, and **they’re** means **they are**. The spelling alone can be hard to hear, so choose by meaning and grammar rather than sound.

A useful first step is to read the sentence aloud with a replacement. If *they are* fits, use *they’re*. If the sentence points to a location or says that something exists, use *there*. If it identifies something connected to a person or group, use *their*. These checks are reliable in ordinary contexts, but consider what the whole sentence means before settling on a choice.

## Their shows possession or connection

**Their** is the possessive form of *they*. It usually comes before a noun and indicates that something belongs to, is associated with, or is done by the people or things referred to. Merriam-Webster describes *their* as the possessive form of *they* and notes that it can refer to what belongs to or relates to them ([“How to Use They’re, There, and Their”](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/how-to-use-theyre-there-their)).

- The hikers packed **their** lunches before sunrise.
- The museum updated **their** visitor map. *(Here, “their” refers to the museum as an organization.)*
- Each student submitted **their** own sketch.

### The substitution test for their

Ask, “Whose?” or try replacing *their* with **my**, **your**, or **his or her**—whichever makes sense for the intended referent. In “The neighbors painted **their** fence,” the fence belongs to or is associated with the neighbors. Replacing *their* with *they are* gives “The neighbors painted they are fence,” which does not work.

This is a meaning test, not a rule that *their* can only mark literal ownership. In “The team changed **their** strategy,” the strategy is connected to the team, even though it is not a physical possession.

## There points or introduces

**There** has several related uses. It can point to a place: “Leave the parcel **there**.” It can also appear at the start of a sentence to say that something exists or is present: “**There** are two parcels by the door.” Cambridge Grammar groups the place use and the *there is/there are* construction in its guidance on [there, their, and they’re](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/there-their-or-they-re).

- The spare key is **there**, beneath the flowerpot.
- **There** is a note on the refrigerator.
- **There** were several reasons to reschedule.

### The substitution test for there

For a place, try **here**, **in that spot**, or another location phrase. “Set the bowl **there**” can become “Set the bowl **on the counter**,” if that is the place intended.

For an existence sentence, try **exists**, **is present**, or **can be found** to see whether the sentence is about something being available or present. “**There** is a seat near the window” means a seat is available in that location. The replacement will not always preserve the sentence’s natural rhythm, but it can clarify the function.

Not every use of *there* names a physical spot. In “**There** seems to be a misunderstanding,” it introduces the fact or situation being described. In expressions such as “I’ll be **there** for you,” it can mean support or availability, rather than a literal location. Follow the sentence’s meaning, not just a rule that *there* always answers “where?”

## They’re is short for they are

**They’re** is a contraction: the apostrophe marks the letters omitted from *they are*. Expand it to those two words. If the expanded sentence makes sense, *they’re* is the right form.

- **They’re** waiting outside. → **They are** waiting outside.
- I think **they’re** ready for the next step. → I think **they are** ready for the next step.
- The curtains are old, but **they’re** still in good shape. → The curtains are old, but **they are** still in good shape.

The word can refer to people, animals, or things. In every case, the substitution remains *they are*. Do not use an apostrophe to indicate possession in *their*: *they’re jacket* is incorrect if you mean a jacket associated with them. Write *their jacket*.

## Singular they: one person, the same three spellings

*They* can refer to more than one person, but it is also used for one person when that person’s gender is unknown or not specified, and by people who use *they/them* pronouns. Cambridge’s [dictionary entry for *they*](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/they) includes examples where *they* refers to one person whose identity has been mentioned in the sentence. Merriam-Webster explains that singular *they* takes the verb forms ordinarily used with *they*: **they are**, **they have**, and **they were** ([“‘They is’ or ‘they are’”](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/singular-nonbinary-they-is-or-they-are)).

For example: “A guest left **their** umbrella. **They’re** welcome to collect it; **there** is a lost-property desk by reception.” The guest is one person, but the three spellings still perform their usual jobs:

- **their** umbrella: the umbrella is associated with the guest.
- **they’re** welcome: *they are* welcome.
- **there** is a desk: the sentence says that a desk is present.

The number of people does not decide whether you need *their*, *there*, or *they’re*. Meaning and sentence structure do. In formal writing, follow any applicable house style for pronouns, but don’t “correct” a person’s stated pronouns by changing their reference.

## Common errors—and a quick editing routine

**Incorrect:** “Their going to bring there instruments.”  
**Correct:** “**They’re** going to bring **their** instruments.”  
The first word expands to *they are*; the second identifies whose instruments.

**Incorrect:** “Put you’re bags over their.”  
**Correct:** “Put **your** bags over **there**.”  
This sentence also confuses *your* (possessive) with *you’re* (*you are*). The location is *there*.

**Incorrect:** “There jackets are hanging by the door.”  
**Correct:** “**Their** jackets are hanging by the door.”  
The jackets are associated with the people being discussed.

When proofreading, use this three-question routine:

1. Can I expand the word to **they are**? If yes, write **they’re**.
2. Does it point to a place, or introduce something that exists or is present? Use **there**.
3. Does it come before a noun and show whose or which group’s? Use **their**.

If a sentence remains confusing, rewrite it briefly to make the intended meaning explicit.
