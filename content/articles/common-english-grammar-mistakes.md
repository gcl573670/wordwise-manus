English grammar errors are easy to miss when a sentence sounds familiar or a spell-checker accepts every word. A useful edit starts by checking what each word does in context: who performs the verb, what a pronoun refers to, and whether each clause forms a complete thought. The examples below show common problems and direct ways to revise them. For more practice, explore [WordWise Hub’s grammar topics](/topics/grammar) and [basic grammar checker](/tools/basic-grammar-checker).

## Agreement, pronouns, and modifiers

### 1. A nearby noun distracts from subject–verb agreement

**Incorrect:** The basket of apples *are* on the counter.  
**Correct:** The basket of apples *is* on the counter.

The subject is *basket*, not the closer plural noun *apples*. Match the verb to the subject, ignoring intervening phrases. Purdue OWL also notes that indefinite pronouns such as *everyone* generally take singular verbs: “Everyone **has** a turn.” With *or* or *nor*, the nearer subject usually guides agreement. [Purdue OWL’s agreement guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/subject_verb_agreement.html) explains these patterns and related exceptions.

### 2. A plural-looking collective noun takes a singular verb

**Incorrect:** The committee *are* meeting today.  
**Correct:** The committee *is* meeting today.

In standard American usage, a collective noun such as *committee* or *team* usually takes a singular verb when the group acts as a unit. If the sentence emphasizes individual members, recast it: “The committee members **are** meeting today.”

### 3. Subject and object pronouns are mixed up

**Incorrect:** Maya and *me* finished the report.  
**Correct:** Maya and *I* finished the report.

Use subject forms (*I, she, we, they*) for subjects and object forms (*me, her, us, them*) for objects of verbs or prepositions: “The editor thanked Maya and **me**.” To check a compound phrase, temporarily remove the other name: “Me finished” sounds wrong; “I finished” works. [Purdue OWL’s pronoun-case guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/pronouns/pronoun_case.html) covers these forms.

### 4. A pronoun’s case is chosen by its role inside the clause

**Incorrect:** Give the form to whoever is in charge.  
**Correct:** Give the form to whomever you choose.

In the correct version, *whomever* is the object of *choose*. But “Give it to **whoever is in charge**” is also correct: *whoever* is the subject of *is*, even though the whole clause follows *to*. Identify the pronoun’s role within its own clause instead of relying only on the surrounding preposition.

### 5. A pronoun has an unclear or mismatched reference

**Unclear:** When the phone reached the tablet, *it* was out of battery.  
**Clear:** The tablet was out of battery when the phone reached it.

A reader should not have to guess which noun *it* represents. Name the intended subject again, or restructure the sentence. Pronouns also need clear number: “Each applicant should bring **their** identification” is a widely used gender-neutral singular construction; a plural rewrite (“Applicants should bring **their** identification”) can avoid a disputed style preference in formal contexts.

### 6. A modifier is placed beside the wrong word

**Unclear:** She served sandwiches to the children on paper plates.  
**Clear:** She served the children sandwiches on paper plates.

The first version can suggest the children are on paper plates. Place a modifier near the word it describes, or rewrite to make the intended relationship unmistakable.

### 7. An introductory modifier dangles

**Incorrect:** After reviewing the draft, several errors stood out.  
**Correct:** After reviewing the draft, I noticed several errors.

The opening phrase implies that the person who reviewed the draft is the subject of the main clause. Give that person a clear subject. A dangling modifier can accidentally assign an action to the wrong thing or person. [Texas A&M’s Writing Center](https://writingcenter.tamu.edu/guides/resources/common-errors.html) illustrates both misplaced and dangling modifiers.

## Complete sentences and clear structure

### 8. A dependent clause is punctuated as a full sentence

**Fragment:** Although the deadline was close.  
**Correct:** Although the deadline was close, we checked every citation.

*Although the deadline was close* leaves the reader waiting for the main idea. Attach the dependent clause to an independent clause, or rewrite it as a complete sentence. Fragments can be deliberate for emphasis in informal or creative writing, but in formal prose they may read as accidental. [Purdue OWL’s fragment guide](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/sentence_fragments.html) gives examples and revision options.

### 9. Two complete sentences are joined with only a comma

**Incorrect:** The bus was late, we missed the opening.  
**Correct:** The bus was late, so we missed the opening.

This is a comma splice. Join closely related independent clauses with a comma and an appropriate coordinating conjunction, use a semicolon, or separate them with a period. Choose a conjunction that conveys the intended relationship; *so* signals a result here.

### 10. Two independent clauses run together without punctuation

**Incorrect:** The shop closed early we returned the next day.  
**Correct:** The shop closed early, so we returned the next day.

A run-on is not simply a long sentence: it occurs when complete clauses lack suitable punctuation or a connector. A period is another simple fix: “The shop closed early. We returned the next day.” Texas A&M’s [common-errors guide](https://writingcenter.tamu.edu/guides/resources/common-errors.html) explains repairs for comma splices and run-ons.

### 11. A list changes grammatical form midway

**Uneven:** The course teaches planning, drafting, and how to revise.  
**Parallel:** The course teaches planning, drafting, and revising.

Keep items in a series in matching grammatical forms. Parallel structure makes the relationship among ideas easier to follow; here, three *-ing* forms form a consistent list.

### 12. A tense shifts without a reason

**Inconsistent:** We checked the figures and discover a missing entry.  
**Consistent:** We checked the figures and discovered a missing entry.

Keep verbs in a consistent time frame unless the events themselves change time. A shift can be correct when meaning requires it: “We checked the figures and now understand the pattern.”

## Confusing words and forms

Many errors below are not misspellings: each choice is a real English word, so context determines whether it fits. The distinctions are supported by Northern Illinois University’s [commonly confused words guide](https://www.niu.edu/writing-tutorial/grammar/commonly-confused-words.shtml).

### 13. *Your* and *you’re*

**Incorrect:** *You’re* umbrella is by the door.  
**Correct:** *Your* umbrella is by the door.

*Your* shows possession. *You’re* means *you are*: “**You’re** carrying an umbrella.” Try substituting *you are*; if the sentence breaks, choose *your*.

### 14. *Their*, *there*, and *they’re*

**Incorrect:** *Their* waiting over *there* with *they’re* bags.  
**Correct:** *They’re* waiting over *there* with *their* bags.

*They’re* means *they are*; *there* points to a place or introduces a clause (“There are two seats”); *their* is possessive. Spell-check may not catch a wrong choice because all three are valid words.

### 15. *Its* and *it’s*

**Incorrect:** The museum changed *it’s* hours.  
**Correct:** The museum changed *its* hours.

*Its* is possessive; *it’s* contracts *it is* or *it has*. Substitute *it is*: “The museum changed it is hours” fails, so use *its*.

### 16. *Affect* and *effect*

**Incorrect:** The new schedule will *effect* my sleep.  
**Correct:** The new schedule will *affect* my sleep.

Usually, *affect* is a verb meaning influence, and *effect* is a noun meaning result: “The change had an **effect**.” There are less common exceptions—*effect* can be a verb meaning bring about, and *affect* has specialized uses—so check the sentence’s intended meaning.

### 17. *Fewer* and *less*

**Incorrect:** We have *less* chairs than last year.  
**Correct:** We have *fewer* chairs than last year.

Use *fewer* with countable items and *less* with amounts or mass nouns: “fewer chairs,” “less time.” Usage can vary in fixed expressions and measurements, so follow the convention appropriate to the context.

### 18. *Then* and *than*

**Incorrect:** The walk was longer *then* we expected.  
**Correct:** The walk was longer *than* we expected.

Use *than* for comparison. *Then* refers to time or sequence: “We ate, then walked.”

### 19. *Who’s* and *whose*

**Incorrect:** *Who’s* coat is hanging here?  
**Correct:** *Whose* coat is hanging here?

*Who’s* means *who is* or *who has*. *Whose* asks about possession. “**Who’s** arriving?” means “Who is arriving?”

### 20. *Could have* and *could of*

**Incorrect:** I *could of* called earlier.  
**Correct:** I *could have* called earlier.

In speech, *could’ve* can sound like “could of,” but the standard written form is *could have* or its contraction *could’ve*. The same applies to *should have* and *would have*.

## A practical proofreading pass

Read a draft once for sentence structure: locate each subject and verb, check that every sentence expresses a complete thought, and look for clauses joined only by commas. Then inspect pronouns and modifiers for clear references. Finally, check commonly confused words in context rather than trusting spelling alone. The [WordWise Hub word counter](/tools/word-counter) can help you review length, while the [dictionary](/dictionary) can clarify a word’s meaning and use. If two alternatives seem close, compare their actual meanings before swapping one for the other.
