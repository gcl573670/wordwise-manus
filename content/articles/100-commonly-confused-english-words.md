# 100 Commonly Confused English Words

Words that look or sound alike can do very different jobs in a sentence. The pairs below cover spelling traps, similar meanings, and easily mixed-up grammar. Learn each word in context rather than relying on a shortcut: some common rules have exceptions, and near-synonyms are not automatically interchangeable. Purdue OWL, Merriam-Webster, and Cambridge Grammar all provide useful usage guidance on these distinctions ([Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/spelling_common_words_that_sound_alike.html); [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/commonly-confused-words-list); [Cambridge Grammar](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/easily-confused-words)).

## Meaning and word-choice pairs

1. **Affect / effect.** *Affect* usually means influence: “The noise affected my focus.” *Effect* is usually a result: “The noise had an effect on my focus.” Exception: *effect* can be a verb meaning bring about: “The team effected a policy change.”
2. **Advice / advise.** *Advice* is a noun for a recommendation: “Her advice helped me revise.” *Advise* is a verb meaning recommend: “I advise you to save a copy.”
3. **Accept / except.** *Accept* means receive or agree to: “Mina accepted the invitation.” *Except* means excluding: “Everyone except Mina arrived.”
4. **Allude / elude.** *Allude* means refer indirectly: “He alluded to an earlier meeting.” *Elude* means escape or remain beyond reach: “The answer eluded us.”
5. **Allusion / illusion.** An *allusion* is an indirect reference: “The poem contains an allusion to winter.” An *illusion* is a misleading appearance or belief: “The mirror created an illusion of space.”
6. **Compliment / complement.** A *compliment* is praise: “She gave the designer a compliment.” A *complement* completes or goes well with something: “The blue scarf complements her coat.”
7. **Principal / principle.** *Principal* can mean a school leader or most important: “The principal welcomed us.” A *principle* is a rule or belief: “Fairness is a principle we value.”
8. **Stationary / stationery.** *Stationary* means not moving: “The bus remained stationary.” *Stationery* means writing materials: “He bought stationery for thank-you notes.”
9. **Conscious / conscience.** *Conscious* means awake or aware: “She was conscious after the fall.” A *conscience* is a sense of right and wrong: “His conscience troubled him.”
10. **Imply / infer.** A speaker *implies* something indirectly: “The note implies that plans changed.” A listener *infers* a conclusion from evidence: “I inferred that the event was canceled.”
11. **Emigrate / immigrate.** *Emigrate* means leave one country to settle elsewhere: “Her grandparents emigrated from Italy.” *Immigrate* means enter a country to settle: “They immigrated to Canada.” The viewpoint determines which verb fits.
12. **Ensure / insure.** *Ensure* means make certain: “Please ensure the door is locked.” *Insure* usually means arrange financial protection: “They insured the house against fire.” In some contexts, *insure* is also used more broadly for making something certain; choose *ensure* when no insurance is involved.
13. **Economic / economical.** *Economic* relates to an economy: “The report tracks economic growth.” *Economical* means avoiding waste or expense: “A compact car can be economical to run.”
14. **Historic / historical.** *Historic* describes something important in history: “The treaty was a historic turning point.” *Historical* means connected with the past or its study: “She reads historical records.” A historical event is not necessarily historic.
15. **Continual / continuous.** *Continual* often describes something repeated with pauses: “Continual interruptions slowed the call.” *Continuous* means unbroken: “A continuous tone sounded for a minute.” Context sometimes blurs this distinction, so specify the pattern when it matters.
16. **Especially / specially.** *Especially* means particularly: “I especially liked the final chapter.” *Specially* often means for a particular purpose: “The case was specially designed for the instrument.” They can overlap in some uses, but the emphasis differs.
17. **Adapt / adopt.** *Adapt* means adjust: “We adapted the recipe for two.” *Adopt* means take up or make legally one’s own: “The committee adopted the proposal.”
18. **Device / devise.** A *device* is a tool or mechanism: “The device measures room temperature.” *Devise* means plan or invent: “They devised a safer route.”
19. **Collaborate / corroborate.** *Collaborate* means work together: “The artists collaborated on a mural.” *Corroborate* means support a claim with evidence: “The records corroborate her account.”
20. **Desert / dessert.** A *desert* is a dry region: “The caravan crossed the desert.” *Dessert* is the sweet course: “We shared fruit for dessert.” *Desert* also has a verb meaning abandon, as in “The crew deserted the ship.”

## Spelling and sound-alike pairs

21. **Its / it’s.** *Its* is possessive: “The cat chased its toy.” *It’s* contracts *it is* or *it has*: “It’s ready”; “It’s been repaired.” Test by substituting *it is* or *it has*.
22. **Your / you’re.** *Your* shows possession: “Your keys are here.” *You’re* means *you are*: “You’re early.”
23. **Whose / who’s.** *Whose* asks who owns something: “Whose coat is this?” *Who’s* means *who is* or *who has*: “Who’s joining us?”
24. **Their / there.** *Their* indicates belonging to them: “Their garden is open.” *There* points to a place or introduces a clause: “Put the box there”; “There are two seats left.” A different commonly confused form, *they’re*, means *they are*; do not use it as a possessive.
25. **Than / then.** *Than* marks comparison: “This path is shorter than the road.” *Then* refers to time, sequence, or consequence: “We ate, then walked.”
26. **Weather / whether.** *Weather* is atmospheric conditions: “The weather turned cold.” *Whether* introduces alternatives or uncertainty: “I’m unsure whether the shop is open.”
27. **Hear / here.** *Hear* means perceive sound: “I can hear the train.” *Here* means this place: “Please wait here.”
28. **Know / no.** *Know* means have information or understanding: “I know the answer.” *No* expresses refusal or negation: “There is no milk.”
29. **Break / brake.** *Break* means separate or interrupt: “Be careful not to break the glass.” A *brake* slows or stops a vehicle: “She pressed the brake.”
30. **Peace / piece.** *Peace* is calm or freedom from conflict: “The neighbors made peace.” A *piece* is a portion: “May I have a piece of bread?”
31. **Write / right.** *Write* means form words: “Please write your address.” *Right* can mean correct, the opposite of left, or an entitlement: “Your answer is right.”
32. **Role / roll.** A *role* is a part or function: “He played the lead role.” *Roll* can mean turn over or a small loaf: “Roll the dough”; “She ate a roll.”
33. **Loose / lose.** *Loose* means not firmly attached or tight: “One button is loose.” *Lose* means fail to keep or win: “Don’t lose the receipt.”
34. **Lead / led.** *Lead* is the present-tense verb meaning guide: “I lead the morning walk.” *Led* is its past tense: “I led it yesterday.” *Lead* is also a metal noun, pronounced differently.
35. **Medal / meddle.** A *medal* is an award: “She earned a medal.” *Meddle* means interfere: “Please don’t meddle in their plans.”
36. **Council / counsel.** A *council* is a group that meets or governs: “The council approved the park.” *Counsel* means advice or a legal adviser: “The lawyer offered counsel.”
37. **Personal / personnel.** *Personal* means private or individual: “That is a personal choice.” *Personnel* refers to an organization’s staff: “All personnel must sign in.”
38. **Precede / proceed.** *Precede* means come before: “A short briefing will precede lunch.” *Proceed* means continue or move forward: “After the pause, we proceeded.”
39. **Eminent / imminent.** *Eminent* means distinguished or prominent: “An eminent scientist gave the lecture.” *Imminent* means about to happen: “A storm is imminent.”
40. **Hoard / horde.** A *hoard* is a stored collection: “The attic held a hoard of letters.” A *horde* is a large crowd: “A horde of fans filled the square.”

## Grammar and context pairs

41. **Fewer / less.** Use *fewer* most often with countable items: “Fewer than ten tickets remain.” Use *less* with amounts not normally counted individually: “We need less water.” In everyday English, *less* also commonly appears with measures and quantities—“less than five miles”—so the countability guide is not an absolute ban.
42. **Farther / further.** *Farther* often refers to physical distance: “We walked farther along the shore.” *Further* often means additional or more: “We need further evidence.” Many speakers use *further* for distance too, so this is a helpful preference, not a rigid rule ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/commonly-confused-words-list)).
43. **Lay / lie.** *Lay* means put something down and takes an object: “Lay the blanket on the grass.” *Lie* means recline and takes no object: “I lie down after lunch.” The past tense of *lie* (recline) is *lay*: “Yesterday I lay down early.”
44. **Raise / rise.** *Raise* means lift something and usually takes an object: “Raise your hand.” *Rise* means move upward and does not take an object: “The sun will rise soon.”
45. **Lend / borrow.** The lender *lends* something to someone: “Could you lend me a pen?” The receiver *borrows* it from someone: “May I borrow your pen?”
46. **Bring / take.** *Bring* generally describes movement toward the speaker, listener, or relevant destination: “Bring your notes to class.” *Take* generally describes movement away from that point: “Take the empty cups to the kitchen.” The reference point in the conversation matters.
47. **Among / between.** *Between* is usual for two distinct parties or for clearly defined individual relationships: “The agreement is between the three neighbors.” *Among* means within a group considered collectively: “She felt at ease among friends.” Avoid the oversimplified claim that *between* only applies to two.
48. **Beside / besides.** *Beside* means next to: “Sit beside the window.” *Besides* means in addition to or apart from: “Besides tea, we serve coffee.”
49. **Everyday / every day.** *Everyday* is an adjective meaning ordinary: “These are my everyday shoes.” *Every day* is a time phrase meaning each day: “I walk every day.”
50. **Maybe / may be.** *Maybe* is an adverb meaning perhaps: “Maybe the parcel will arrive today.” *May be* is a verb phrase expressing possibility: “The parcel may be delayed.”

## Keep the distinction in context

When two forms still seem interchangeable, check the part of speech and the sentence’s intended meaning.
