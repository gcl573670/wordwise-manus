“Your ready to begin” and “You’re ready to begin” may sound identical, but only one is right in that sentence. The distinction is grammatical: **your** is a possessive determiner, while **you’re** is a contraction of **you are**. A quick substitution test usually reveals which one fits; a closer look at the sentence helps when the result is less obvious.

## The difference between *your* and *you’re*

Use **your** to connect something with the person or people being addressed. It commonly appears directly before a noun: *your ticket*, *your idea*, *your turn*. The relationship need not be literal ownership. In *your question*, the question is associated with the person asking it; in *your usual seat*, the seat is the one normally used by that person. Merriam-Webster also notes uses such as *your typical teenager*, where *your* can work almost like *the*. [Merriam-Webster’s definition of *your*](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/your) includes relationships beyond straightforward possession.

Use **you’re** when you mean **you are**. It can be followed by an adjective (*you’re ready*), a verb ending in *-ing* (*you’re waiting*), or another phrase that completes the sentence (*you’re the first person here*). The apostrophe marks the shortened form; it does not show ownership. Merriam-Webster’s usage guide confirms the expansion test and distinguishes the two forms. |---|---|---|
| **your** | Relates a noun to the person addressed | *Your coat is by the door.* |
| **you’re** | Stands for *you are* | *You’re standing by the door.* |

## Use the “you are” substitution test

Replace **you’re** or **your** with the two words **you are**. If the sentence still says what you intend and is grammatically complete, use **you’re**. If that substitution does not work, and the sentence needs a word relating a noun to the person addressed, use **your**. This is the standard practical check described by Merriam-Webster.

- *You’re bringing your notes.* → *You are bringing your notes.* Both substitutions have a job: *you are* tells what the person is doing; *your* connects the notes to that person.
- *Your notes are on the desk.* → *You are notes are on the desk* does not work. The sentence needs the possessive determiner before *notes*.
- *I think you’re right.* → *I think you are right* is grammatical, so **you’re** is correct.
- *I like your answer.* → *I like you are answer* does not work; **your** modifies *answer*.

Do not stop at “the sentence sounds fine.” Both spellings are often pronounced alike, so sound alone cannot settle the choice. Look at the written sentence and test its grammar.

## Examples in everyday sentences

**Your** usually has a noun after it, though that noun may have modifiers between the determiner and the main noun:

- *Please save your seat.*
- *Your carefully revised draft is in the folder.*
- *The team appreciated your help.*
- *Is that your umbrella, or did someone leave it here?*
- *Your of-the-moment playlist is already playing.*

In each example, *your* helps identify or characterize a noun: *seat*, *draft*, *help*, *umbrella*, or *playlist*. As the University of Prince Edward Island Writing Centre’s guide to common confusing words also identifies, *your* is a possessive determiner, distinct from *you’re*, the contraction of *you are*. **You’re** can introduce a description, an action, or an identity:

- *You’re unusually quiet today.* → *You are unusually quiet today.*
- *You’re meeting us after lunch.* → *You are meeting us after lunch.*
- *If you’re the caller, please leave your number.* → *If you are the caller, please leave your number.*
- *You’re allowed to change your mind.* → *You are allowed to change your mind.*
- *You’re one of the few people who noticed your name was missing.*

These examples show why a sentence can correctly contain both words. In *You’re allowed to change your mind*, **you’re** means *you are*; **your** relates *mind* to the person addressed.

## Tricky cases to check carefully

### A noun does not always follow immediately after *your*

A descriptive word or phrase may sit between *your* and the noun: *your recently published article*, *your next big opportunity*. That does not make **your** a contraction. Identify the noun the phrase describes, then ask whether *your* connects that noun with the addressee.

### *Your* does not always mean literal ownership

In *your appointment*, the appointment is associated with you, not something you own. In *your side of the story*, the connection is about perspective. The test is not “Do you possess this?” but “Does this form appropriately relate the noun to you?” Dictionary definitions include “of or relating to” the person addressed, not only ownership. [Merriam-Webster Dictionary](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/your)

### The substitution test should preserve the sentence’s meaning

The test is a diagnostic, not permission to insert *you are* anywhere it produces a sequence of familiar words. For example, *You’re report is clear* becomes *You are report is clear*, which is ungrammatical; the intended phrase is *Your report is clear*. Conversely, *You’re a careful editor* expands naturally to *You are a careful editor*. Check both grammar and intended meaning.

### Formal writing may favor the expanded form

**You’re** is a standard contraction, not a misspelling. Still, a writer may choose **you are** in a formal document, for emphasis, or to maintain a consistent tone. That stylistic choice does not change the distinction: **your** cannot replace *you are*, and **you’re** cannot replace a possessive determiner.

### Neither form is interchangeable with *yours*

Compare *your notebook* with *The notebook is yours*. **Your** comes before a noun; **yours** can stand on its own in this possessive role. Neither means *you are*. ## A short editing method

When proofreading, use these steps:

1. **Read the entire sentence.** Find the spot where you wrote *your* or *you’re*.
2. **Expand the contraction candidate.** Replace it with *you are*. If that creates the intended grammatical sentence, choose **you’re**.
3. **Check for a noun relationship.** If *you are* fails and the phrase needs a determiner connected to a noun—such as *your schedule* or *your unusually clear explanation*—choose **your**.
4. **Read the corrected sentence once more.** Confirm the meaning, not just the spelling. Pay special attention to sentences that use both forms.

For example, edit *Your going to need you’re key* one part at a time. *You are going to need* works, so the opening should be **You’re**. The key relates to the person addressed, so the final word should be **your**: *You’re going to need your key.* A browser-based [basic grammar checker](/tools/basic-grammar-checker) can help flag possible issues, but the substitution test lets you verify this specific distinction yourself.

## The quick rule

Choose **you’re** when you mean **you are**. Choose **your** when you need a possessive determiner relating a noun to the person addressed. In doubt, expand the contraction, check that the grammar and meaning hold, and then read the whole sentence again.
