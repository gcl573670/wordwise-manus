# Common English Phrases and Their Meanings

Everyday English relies on short expressions whose effect depends on the situation, relationship, and delivery. “Could you help?” is a request, while “Could you help?” said with a rising, surprised tone might question someone’s ability. The phrases below are grouped by what speakers usually want to do. Examples are original; meanings describe common conversational uses, not rules that fit every setting.

If you are learning an expression, notice whether it starts a conversation, asks for an action, responds to news, connects ideas, or checks understanding. Related vocabulary can be explored through WordWise Hub’s [dictionary](/dictionary), [idioms](/idioms), and [reference articles](/topics/reference).

## Greetings and opening a conversation

### 1. “How’s it going?”
A relaxed way to ask how someone is doing. It suits informal conversation with peers or familiar people; a brief answer is normal. **Example:** “How’s it going? Did the new class start today?”

### 2. “Good to see you.”
A warm greeting that signals pleasure at meeting again. It is friendly and works in both casual and many workplace settings. **Example:** “Good to see you, Maya. How was your trip?”

### 3. “How have you been?”
A question about someone’s life since you last met, rather than just their present mood. Use it when there has been a gap between meetings. **Example:** “How have you been? I heard you moved to Bristol.”

### 4. “Nice to meet you.”
A polite expression for an introduction, usually when meeting someone for the first time. It can sound conventional, so follow it with a genuine question if you want to continue talking. **Example:** “Nice to meet you, Leon. Which team do you work with?”

### 5. “What’s up?”
An informal greeting or a way to ask what is happening. Depending on context, it may be a real question or simply an easygoing hello. **Example:** “Hey, what’s up? We saved you a seat.”

### 6. “Long time no see.”
A casual way to acknowledge that you have not seen someone for a while. It is conversational rather than formal. **Example:** “Long time no see! Are you still working near the station?”

## Requests and polite ways to ask

“Could you” and “would you” are common polite request forms. The British Council contrasts them with the generally less polite “can you” and “will you,” while Cambridge notes that “could” can be more polite than “can.” Tone, relationship, and circumstances still matter: a direct form may be perfectly appropriate between close colleagues, while a softer form can be useful when asking a stranger. [British Council guidance on requests](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/requests-offers-invitations) [Cambridge Grammar on can, could, and may](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/can-could-or-may)

### 7. “Could you help me with this?”
A polite request for assistance. It leaves room for the other person to say whether they can help. **Example:** “Could you help me with this form? I’m not sure which box to tick.”

### 8. “Would you mind…?”
A considerate way to ask someone to do something. Follow it with a verb ending in *-ing*: “Would you mind opening the window?” A positive answer can be confusing: “No, not at all” means the person does not mind. **Example:** “Would you mind moving your bag so I can sit down?”

### 9. “Can I ask you a favor?”
A conversational preface to a request. It alerts the listener that you need something, but does not explain what the favor is yet. **Example:** “Can I ask you a favor? Would you check the address before I send this?”

### 10. “Could I have…?”
A polite way to ask for an item or service, especially in a shop or café. It is not a literal question about ability. **Example:** “Could I have a glass of water, please?”

### 11. “Do you happen to know…?”
A softened way to ask for information, often used when the other person may not know the answer. **Example:** “Do you happen to know when the library closes?”

### 12. “Whenever you have a moment…”
A tactful way to signal that a task is not urgent. Avoid it if you actually need an immediate response, since the listener may reasonably delay. **Example:** “Whenever you have a moment, could you send me the meeting notes?”

## Responses to people and situations

### 13. “That sounds good.”
A positive response to a suggestion or plan. It expresses approval, though it may not by itself confirm every detail. **Example:** “Lunch at one? That sounds good. Which café?”

### 14. “No problem.”
A casual way to say that a request or thanks is not an inconvenience. It is friendly in everyday settings; for a formal message, “You’re welcome” may feel more measured. **Example:** “Thanks for holding the door.” “No problem.”

### 15. “I’m not sure.”
A cautious answer that signals uncertainty rather than a definite refusal. Add a reason or next step to make your meaning clear. **Example:** “I’m not sure we can finish by noon; I’ll check the delivery time.”

### 16. “That makes sense.”
A response showing that an explanation seems logical or understandable. It does not necessarily mean you agree with the decision. **Example:** “That makes sense; the later train avoids the transfer.”

### 17. “I see what you mean.”
A way to acknowledge another person’s point. It can show understanding without saying that you share their opinion. **Example:** “I see what you mean about the title—it sounds more formal than the rest.”

### 18. “Thanks anyway.”
A polite response when help or an offer was made but did not solve the problem. Depending on voice, it can sound appreciative or slightly disappointed. **Example:** “The shop is closed? Thanks anyway for checking.”

## Transitions that connect ideas

### 19. “By the way…”
Introduces an additional topic, often one that is not central to the current discussion. It can also be used to bring up a reminder. **Example:** “By the way, your parcel is at reception.”

### 20. “On the other hand…”
Signals a contrasting consideration or an alternative perspective. It is useful in discussion and writing when the contrast is meaningful. **Example:** “The flat is close to work. On the other hand, it has very little storage.”

### 21. “For example…”
Introduces a specific case that illustrates a general point. The example should directly support the idea just mentioned. **Example:** “We can reduce waste in small ways. For example, the office can reuse shipping boxes.”

### 22. “In that case…”
Connects a previous fact or condition to a resulting decision or suggestion. **Example:** “The bus stops running at ten? In that case, let’s take a taxi back.”

### 23. “Anyway…”
Often marks a return to the main point, a change of subject, or an ending. Its effect depends on intonation; said abruptly, it may sound impatient. **Example:** “Anyway, we’ve chosen a date—let’s book the room.”

### 24. “First of all…”
Introduces the first item in an explanation or sequence. It can help listeners follow instructions, but may sound overly emphatic if no further points follow. **Example:** “First of all, save a copy of the original file.”

## Clarification and checking understanding

When you did not hear something, ask for repetition. When you heard the words but are unsure what they mean, ask a specific follow-up. “Excuse me?” can politely ask someone to repeat themselves, but Cambridge also notes it has other uses, including before disagreeing and apologizing for an unintended annoyance. Context and intonation help distinguish these meanings. [Cambridge Dictionary: “excuse me”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/dictionary/english/excuse-me). The [British Council’s checking-understanding practice](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding) likewise focuses on expressions speakers use when communication breaks down.

### 25. “Could you say that again?”
A courteous request for repetition when you missed or did not catch the words. **Example:** “Could you say that again? The connection cut out.”

### 26. “What do you mean by…?”
Asks someone to explain a particular word or idea. Naming the unclear part makes the question more useful. **Example:** “What do you mean by ‘flexible hours’—can the start time change?”

### 27. “Do you mean…?”
Checks whether your interpretation is correct. Use it before acting on information that could be understood in more than one way. **Example:** “Do you mean we should email the draft today, not the final version?”

### 28. “Let me make sure I understand.”
Signals that you are about to restate what you heard. It is calm and collaborative, especially for instructions or decisions. **Example:** “Let me make sure I understand: you want the blue samples by Friday.”

### 29. “Could you give me an example?”
Requests a specific illustration when a general explanation is still unclear. **Example:** “Could you give me an example of a suitable file name?”

### 30. “I didn’t quite catch that.”
A tactful way to say you did not hear or understand something. “Quite” softens the statement; it does not mean you caught most of the message for certain. **Example:** “I didn’t quite catch that last date—was it Tuesday or Thursday?”

## Choose the phrase for the relationship and moment

A phrase does not have one fixed level of politeness in every situation. “Can you send it?” may be efficient and natural with a teammate, but a stranger or formal request may call for “Could you send it, please?” Even “please” cannot make every demand sound considerate if the rest of the message is abrupt. Use a greeting when opening an exchange, make requests specific, and ask targeted clarification questions instead of pretending to understand.
