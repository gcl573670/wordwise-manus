English abbreviations make messages shorter, label dates and measurements, and help readers recognize familiar organizations and concepts. But a shortened form that works in a text message may feel unclear or too casual in a report. Meaning also depends on context: *Dr.* commonly means “Doctor,” for example, but titles and conventions can vary by region and style guide.

This guide explains the difference between abbreviations, initialisms, and acronyms, clears up the often-confused *e.g.* and *i.e.*, and gives 36 common forms in context. For more curated reference material, visit WordWise Hub’s [abbreviations](/abbreviations) and [reference](/topics/reference) pages.

## Abbreviation, initialism, and acronym: what’s the difference?

An **abbreviation** is a shortened form of a word or phrase. *Ave.* for *avenue* and *approx.* for *approximately* are abbreviations. The term also covers forms built from initial letters.

An **initialism** is formed from the initial letters of a phrase and spoken as separate letter names: *BBC* (“B-B-C”) and *ATM* (“A-T-M”). An **acronym**, in the narrower sense, is formed from initial letters and pronounced as a word, as in *NASA* or *NATO*. Merriam-Webster notes that *acronym* is also commonly used more broadly for initial-letter forms such as *FBI*. In everyday writing, readers may encounter both usages, so use the more specific term when the pronunciation distinction matters. [Merriam-Webster explains the distinction](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/whats-an-acronym).

Some abbreviations are written with lowercase letters and periods; others use capitals without periods. That choice can depend on the abbreviation, the publication, and the style being followed. For example, *U.S.* and *US* both occur, though a particular style guide may prefer one form in a given context. APA recommends avoiding periods in most all-capital abbreviations, with exceptions such as *U.S.* before a noun; Chicago’s guidance keeps periods in *e.g.* and *i.e.* [Purdue OWL summarizes APA conventions](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/apa_style/apa_formatting_and_style_guide/apa_abbreviations.html). When writing for a class, workplace, or publication, follow its style guide consistently.

## 36 common English abbreviations and meanings

Examples below are original illustrations of typical usage. Some entries are acronyms or initialisms as well as abbreviations; the category labels overlap.

| Short form | Meaning | Context example |
|---|---|---|
| **a.m.** | Before noon; from *ante meridiem* | The call starts at 9 a.m. |
| **p.m.** | After noon; from *post meridiem* | The library closes at 6 p.m. |
| **approx.** | Approximately | The walk takes approx. 20 minutes. |
| **ASAP** | As soon as possible (initialism; often spoken as a word) | Please send the revised schedule ASAP. |
| **Ave.** | Avenue | The studio is on Pine Ave. |
| **B.C. / BCE** | Before Christ / Before the Common Era | The settlement dates to 300 BCE. |
| **c. / ca.** | Circa, meaning approximately, especially for dates | The portrait was made c. 1810. |
| **CEO** | Chief executive officer (initialism) | The CEO addressed staff at the meeting. |
| **DIY** | Do it yourself (initialism) | They used a DIY kit to build the shelf. |
| **Dr.** | Doctor; a title before a name | Dr. Lee will lead the seminar. |
| **e.g.** | For example; introduces examples | Bring a warm layer, e.g., a sweater. |
| **etc.** | And other things of the same kind | Pack a notebook, pens, sticky notes, etc. |
| **ETA** | Estimated time of arrival (initialism) | Her ETA is 4:30 p.m. |
| **FBI** | Federal Bureau of Investigation (initialism) | The report quoted an FBI spokesperson. |
| **ft.** | Foot or feet, a unit of length | The room is 12 ft. wide. |
| **ID** | Identification or identifying document | Show your ID at reception. |
| **i.e.** | That is; introduces a clarification or restatement | The meeting is remote, i.e., everyone joins online. |
| **Inc.** | Incorporated, used in some business names | North Star Design, Inc., opened a new office. |
| **Jr.** | Junior, often following a person's name | The invitation was addressed to Morgan Ellis Jr. |
| **kg** | Kilogram(s), a unit of mass | The parcel weighs 2 kg. |
| **km** | Kilometer(s), a unit of distance | The trailhead is 4 km away. |
| **Ltd.** | Limited, used in some company names | The invoice came from Greenfield Ltd. |
| **max.** | Maximum | Each group may include a max. of eight people. |
| **min.** | Minute(s), or minimum depending on context | Allow 15 min. for the exercise. |
| **Mr.** | Mister, a title | Mr. Patel will introduce the speaker. |
| **Mrs.** | A title traditionally used for a married woman | Mrs. Chen requested a printed copy. |
| **Ms.** | A title for a woman that does not specify marital status | Ms. Rivera is the new editor. |
| **NASA** | National Aeronautics and Space Administration (acronym) | NASA shared images from the mission. |
| **No.** | Number | Please take ticket No. 18. |
| **Ph.D.** | Doctor of Philosophy, an academic degree | He earned a Ph.D. in linguistics. |
| **PO Box** | Post office box | Send the form to PO Box 208. |
| **RSVP** | Please respond; from the French *répondez s’il vous plaît* | The invitation asks guests to RSVP by Friday. |
| **St.** | Street or Saint, depending on context | The shop is at 8 King St.; St. Anne’s is nearby. |
| **U.S. / US** | United States | She moved to the US last year. |
| **vs.** | Versus; against or in comparison with | The article compares print vs. digital dictionaries. |
| **YTD** | Year to date (initialism) | The team’s YTD spending is within budget. |

In the table, *B.C.* and *BCE* express the same broad calendar designation in different conventions; *BCE* avoids explicitly Christian wording. *St.* can mean *Street* or *Saint*, so nearby words and formatting help readers identify which is intended. *min.* can likewise mean *minute* or *minimum*. Spell it out if the context does not make the intended meaning clear.

## E.g. vs. i.e.: examples or explanation?

Use **e.g.** when you are giving one or more examples. The examples illustrate a broader group and do not necessarily list every possible item: “For the workshop, bring a writing tool, e.g., a pencil.”

Use **i.e.** when you are clarifying or restating what came immediately before it: “The final session is asynchronous, i.e., you can complete it on your own time.” The clarification is intended to specify what *asynchronous* means here. The Chicago Manual of Style’s explainer makes this specific-versus-general distinction clear. [Chicago Manual of Style, “I.e., E.g., Etc.”](https://cmosshoptalk.com/2023/04/11/i-e-e-g-etc/)

A useful check: if *for example* fits, choose *e.g.*; if *that is* fits, choose *i.e.* Do not automatically add *etc.* after *e.g.*: “e.g.” already signals that the list is illustrative, so the extra marker is usually redundant. Chicago’s style uses periods and generally places a comma after these forms, but punctuation preferences vary. In formal prose, some editors prefer spelling out *for example* and *that is*, especially outside parentheses, notes, or tables. [Purdue OWL’s APA guidance](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/apa_style/apa_formatting_and_style_guide/apa_abbreviations.html) also demonstrates that abbreviation rules belong to a defined style rather than to one universal punctuation system.

## Make abbreviations easy to understand

Use an abbreviation when it genuinely helps readers, not just because it saves a few characters. For an unfamiliar or specialized term, write it out at first mention and put the shortened form in parentheses if you will use it again: “The community health centre (CHC) opens at eight.” In a short note, defining a rare abbreviation may create more clutter than writing the term in full. APA similarly advises limiting abbreviations to forms that are standard and clear, and recommends defining many nonstandard forms at first use. [Purdue OWL: APA Abbreviations](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/apa_style/apa_formatting_and_style_guide/apa_abbreviations.html)

Think about audience as well as style. *ASAP* is ordinary in many workplace messages, but “as soon as possible” may sound more considerate when a request needs a clear deadline. Spell out an unfamiliar acronym in public-facing writing, and avoid assuming that a technical abbreviation will be familiar to every reader.
