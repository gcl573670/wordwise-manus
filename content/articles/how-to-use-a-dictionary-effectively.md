A dictionary is most useful when you treat it as a guide to a word in context, not as a machine that supplies one interchangeable meaning. A headword may have several senses, grammatical roles, pronunciations, or regional uses. To find the right answer, start with the sentence where you met the word and follow the entry details that match it.

## Start with the sentence, not the first definition

Before searching, note the sentence and ask what job the unfamiliar word is doing. Is it naming a thing, describing an action, or modifying another word? What words appear beside it? Those clues help you choose among senses once you open an entry.

Do not assume the first definition is the right one—or even that every dictionary orders senses the same way. Entries may arrange meanings by commonness or historical order. Merriam-Webster explains that its entries can use either approach, and that the first sense is not automatically the most important or correct. Compare the definition with the whole sentence, then read the example attached to that specific sense. [Merriam-Webster’s guide to dictionary entries](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/how-to-use-the-dictionary)

For example, in “The new software runs on a tablet,” *runs* means “operates,” not “moves quickly.” The subject and surrounding phrase point to the relevant sense. If no definition fits, check whether the word is part of an idiom, phrasal verb, technical expression, or proper name rather than forcing a nearby meaning.

## Use the part of speech to narrow the sense

A part-of-speech label tells you how the entry uses a word: *noun*, *verb*, *adjective*, *adverb*, and so on. One spelling may belong to more than one category, with different meanings or patterns. Compare “They **record** the result” (verb) and “Keep a **record** of the result” (noun). If the sentence requires a verb but you are reading a noun definition, you are in the wrong part of the entry.

Check the grammar around the word, too. A transitive verb usually takes an object (“She **filed** the report”); an intransitive use does not (“The machine **stopped**”). Entries may give patterns or grammar codes that show which construction is expected. A dictionary’s help pages explain its own abbreviations, so consult the key rather than guessing what a code means. See more on sentence structure in [WordWise Hub’s grammar resources](/topics/grammar).

## Read labels as practical warnings

Labels tell you where, when, or how a word is used. Depending on the dictionary, you may see notes such as *informal*, *slang*, *formal*, *dated*, *offensive*, or a regional label such as *British English*. They do not all mean “wrong.” Instead, they help you judge whether a word fits a particular audience and situation. A slang sense might be suitable in a chat but unsuitable in a formal report; a regional term may be unfamiliar to readers elsewhere.

Labels and abbreviations are not perfectly uniform across reference works. Read the dictionary’s own label guide, and notice whether a label applies to the whole word or only one sense. If a choice could sound disrespectful or out of place, check a current entry and usage note before using it. Merriam-Webster describes labels as additional information about a word or sense; Cambridge provides explanations of its own grammar and learner-level codes. [Cambridge Dictionary Help](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/help/)

## Check pronunciation—and the accent

A phonetic transcription represents sounds with symbols; an audio button lets you hear a recorded pronunciation. Cambridge says its entries include International Phonetic Alphabet transcriptions and, in most entries, separate British and American audio. Listen to the version relevant to your context, and compare the transcription if you are learning to recognize sound patterns. [Cambridge Dictionary Help](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/help/)

Pronunciation can vary by region, word sense, or speaker. A single recording is a useful model, not proof that every other variant is incorrect. Pay attention to stress marks, syllable divisions, and any listed alternatives. If you cannot hear a distinction reliably, replay the audio and compare it with an example from a trusted source instead of treating a rough respelling as exact.

## Learn the word in a sentence and its company

Examples show how a definition behaves in context: the words that commonly accompany it, the tone it carries, and the grammar it fits. Read examples under the specific sense you need. Then notice whether the word takes a particular preposition, object, or complement. For instance, *depend* commonly appears with *on* (“The result depends on the weather”). That pattern is part of using the word naturally, not an optional decoration.

These recurring word partnerships are called collocations. A dictionary may include them directly, in phrases, or through linked topics; a learner’s dictionary or collocations reference can make them easier to spot. Cambridge’s entry for *run*, for example, separates senses such as “operate” and “travel,” with examples showing distinct sentence patterns. [Cambridge Dictionary: “run”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/run)

Examples are evidence of a possible use, not permission to swap in any near-synonym. Words that seem close can differ in formality, emotional force, grammar, or typical partners. Check a separate [synonym guide](/synonyms) for alternatives, then verify the candidate word in its own entry and sentence examples.

## Look up inflections and related forms

If the word in your sentence is plural, past tense, or ending in *-ing*, search the base form if the dictionary does not find it automatically. Entries often list inflected forms such as *child/children*, *try/tried*, or *write/wrote/written*. Some forms are irregular, and a spelling change can help you identify the lemma—the form under which the entry is organized.

Also distinguish an inflection from a different word formed with a suffix. *Walked* is an inflected form of *walk*; *walker* is a related noun that usually has its own entry. Check the part of speech and definition for the exact form you plan to use. This is particularly helpful when editing: a word may be correct in one grammatical form but not another. You can pair lookups with the [basic grammar checker](/tools/basic-grammar-checker), while remembering that a tool cannot replace context-sensitive reading.

## Follow cross-references, but verify the destination

Entries may direct you to another spelling, variant, phrase, related word, or sense using links or notes such as “see,” “compare,” or “also.” Follow the link when it offers the form actually used in your sentence. The destination may contain the fuller definition or relevant usage note.

A cross-reference is a navigation aid, not a claim that two terms mean exactly the same thing. Read the destination’s definition and examples before adopting it. For idiomatic expressions, search the complete phrase rather than looking up only one word; explore [idioms](/idioms) when the meaning seems figurative or cannot be composed from the individual words.

## Treat etymology and translation as separate tools

Etymology describes a word’s history and development. It can be fascinating, but an old root or earlier sense does not dictate what the word means now. Merriam-Webster explicitly notes that etymology can be distinct from definition, and the Online Etymology Dictionary describes etymologies as explanations of historical meaning and sound, not definitions. Use the current entry and examples for present-day meaning; use word history to investigate origins, with care about what the record can establish. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/how-to-use-the-dictionary) · [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/)

A bilingual dictionary is a starting point for translation, not a guarantee that a single target-language word fits every sentence. One source-language word may have several senses, and a suggested equivalent may change with register, grammar, or cultural context. Choose the sense in the original sentence first; then examine example phrases and the target-language word’s own entry. For important writing, compare the entire phrase and surrounding sentence, not just a one-word match.

## Make each lookup answer a question

A focused lookup is easier to apply: “Which sense fits here?”, “Is this noun countable?”, “Which pronunciation matches my audience?”, or “Does this verb take *to* or *for*?” Read the relevant definition, label, example, and grammar note, then return to your sentence and reread it. If it still sounds wrong, check another reputable dictionary or a specialized learner or subject dictionary. No single work contains every word, regional variety, or emerging use; even Merriam-Webster notes that an omitted word is not thereby unreal. The goal is not to memorize an entry, but to use evidence from the entry to make a context-sensitive choice.
